Video Poker Pay Tables: Why 9/6 Jacks or Better Matters

In video poker, the pay table sets the house edge, so reading it is the most useful skill you can learn. A "9/6" Jacks or Better game (9 for a full house, 6 for a flush) returns about 99.54% with perfect play, while the common 8/5 version returns about 97.30%, which makes it roughly six times as expensive per hand.

Video poker looks like a slot machine, but it works very differently. Each hand is dealt from a fresh, shuffled 52-card deck, so the chance of every result can be worked out exactly from the rules. That means you can see the long-run cost of a game just by reading its pay table, before you bet a cent. This guide explains how Jacks or Better plays, how to read the pay table, why the 9/6 version matters, and the handful of strategy rules that make the biggest difference.

How Jacks or Better video poker works

Jacks or Better is the original and most common video poker game. A hand plays out in four steps:

  • Bet: choose your coin size and how many coins to play, usually one to five.
  • Deal: you get five cards from a standard 52-card deck.
  • Hold and draw: pick which cards to keep. The rest are replaced from the same deck.
  • Payout: your final five-card hand is paid according to the pay table. The lowest paying hand is a pair of jacks or better (jacks, queens, kings or aces). A pair of tens or lower pays nothing.

There is no dealer hand to beat and no other players. You only have to make the best poker hand you can, and the only decision is which cards to hold. That decision is where skill comes in, and it is why video poker has one of the lowest house edges in the casino when played well.

How to read a video poker pay table

The pay table is the chart at the top of the screen showing what each hand pays per coin bet. Here is the "full-pay" 9/6 Jacks or Better table, with how often each final hand comes up when you play perfect strategy. *The royal flush pays 800 per coin (4,000 coins) only when you bet the maximum five coins. With fewer coins it usually pays 250 per coin. Payouts include your stake: "1" for jacks or better means you get your bet back.

Two things stand out. First, you finish with a paying hand only about 45% of the time, and the most common win (jacks or better) just returns your bet. Second, the royal flush is rare, but at 800 for 1 it still supplies about 2% of the game's total return. Every line on the table adds a share, and the total of all those shares is the game's return to player.

What "9/6" means

Most Jacks or Better games keep the same payouts for four of a kind and above, and for three of a kind and below. The two lines that casinos usually change are the full house and the flush. Players use those two numbers as shorthand: "9/6" means a full house pays 9 and a flush pays 6. "8/5" means 8 and 5. So the quickest way to judge a Jacks or Better game is to look at just those two lines.

Why 9/6 Jacks or Better matters: pay tables compared

Cutting the full house by one coin and the flush by one coin doesn't look like much. Because those hands come up more than once every 100 hands, though, the effect on the return is large. We worked out each version below with the best possible strategy for that pay table, betting five coins:

VersionFull house / flushReturn (perfect play)House edge
Full-pay9 / 699.54%0.46%
Short-pay8 / 597.30%2.70%
Short-pay7 / 596.15%3.85%
Short-pay6 / 595.00%5.00%
9/6, fewer than 5 coins (royal pays 250)9 / 698.37%1.63%

Going from 9/6 to 8/5 raises the house edge from 0.46% to 2.70%, nearly six times as much. A 6/5 game costs more than ten times as much as full pay, and it looks almost identical on screen. The difference only shows up in the small numbers on the pay table, which is why it pays to check them every time.

The last row shows why the coin count matters too. Playing fewer than five coins on the same 9/6 machine cuts the royal flush from 800 to 250 per coin, and the return drops from 99.54% to about 98.37%. If five coins at your chosen coin size is more than you want to bet, a smaller coin size with five coins is usually the cheaper choice.

Even at 99.54%, the game still has a house edge. No strategy removes it. Perfect play just keeps it as small as the pay table allows. For how video poker compares with blackjack, roulette and slots, see our guide to the house edge on every casino game.

Your real result over 800 hands will almost always be far from these averages. A big part of the 99.54% comes from the royal flush, which shows up about once in 40,000 hands. The chance of hitting at least one royal in 10,000 hands is only about 22%. Without one, most sessions end below the average. That swing is why a bankroll budget matters more than the return figure when you decide how much to bring.

Jacks or Better strategy: the decisions that matter most

The return figures above assume perfect strategy, which means always holding the cards that give the highest average payout. Full strategy charts have dozens of lines, but a few rules cover the most common and most costly decisions. From these examples come a few simple rules:

  • Break a flush or straight for four to a royal. Four cards to a royal flush are worth about three times a made flush on average.
  • Keep a high pair over a four-card flush. The pair already pays, and it can improve to two pair, trips or better.
  • Keep any pair over two unsuited high cards. Even a low pair beats holding a king and queen of different suits.
  • Three to a royal beats four to an open-ended straight. The royal draw also brings flush, straight and high-pair chances.
  • Hold a lone jack, queen, king or ace rather than drawing five new cards.

Strategy is specific to the game and the pay table. These rules are for Jacks or Better; games with wild cards or bonus payouts for four of a kind need their own strategy. Like blackjack basic strategy, the point is to stop giving away value through avoidable mistakes, not to get an edge over the game.

Common mistakes

  • Not checking the pay table. Two machines with the same name can differ by several percentage points. Look at the full house and flush lines first.
  • Betting fewer than five coins. The royal flush bonus only pays on a full bet, and that costs about 1.2 percentage points of return.
  • Holding a kicker. Keeping an ace or other card next to a pair lowers the average return. With a pair, hold just the pair.
  • Keeping a made flush or straight with four to a royal. It feels safe, but it gives up a lot of value.
  • Thinking a machine is "due". Each hand is dealt from a freshly shuffled deck. Past hands don't change the next one.
  • Playing faster to "earn" more. More hands per hour means more total bets, so your average loss per hour goes up even on a 9/6 game.

When to walk away

Set a loss limit and a time limit before you start, and stop when you reach either one. Video poker can be played very quickly, so an hour of play adds up to a lot of total bets even at small stakes. Walk away if you are chasing a royal flush to win back earlier losses, raising your coin size after a bad run, or playing with money meant for bills. A low house edge makes video poker cheaper entertainment than many games, but it is still entertainment, not income.
