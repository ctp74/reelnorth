Baccarat Strategy: Banker, Player and Tie Bets Explained

The only baccarat strategy backed by the maths is to bet on the banker: in a standard eight-deck game with a 5% commission, it has a house edge of about 1.06%, against 1.24% for the player bet. The tie bet looks tempting at 8 to 1, but its house edge is about 14.4%, so it is the bet to skip.

Baccarat is one of the simplest casino games to play, because you make no decisions once the cards are dealt. The dealer follows fixed drawing rules, and your only choice is which bet to make and how much to stake. That makes the bet you pick the whole of baccarat strategy. This guide explains how a hand plays out, where each house edge comes from, what "no-commission" tables change, and why scorecards and betting systems don't help.

How a baccarat hand works

Two hands are dealt: one called Player and one called Banker. Despite the names, you are not the player and the casino is not the banker. They are just two hands, and you bet on which one will finish closer to 9, or on a tie.

  • Card values: aces count 1, cards 2 to 9 count their face value, and tens, jacks, queens and kings count 0.
  • Hand totals: only the last digit counts. A 7 and an 8 make 15, which counts as 5.
  • Naturals: if either hand totals 8 or 9 on its first two cards, both hands stand and the hand is settled.
  • Payouts: a winning player bet pays even money. A winning banker bet pays even money minus a 5% commission, so a $10 bet wins $9.50. A tie usually pays 8 to 1, and banker and player bets push (are returned) on a tie.

The drawing rules

If there is no natural, the Player hand draws a third card on a total of 0 to 5 and stands on 6 or 7. The Banker hand then follows this table:

Banker totalBanker draws when…
0, 1 or 2Always draws
3Draws unless Player's third card was an 8
4Draws if Player's third card was 2 to 7
5Draws if Player's third card was 4 to 7
6Draws if Player's third card was 6 or 7
7Stands

If the Player hand stood on two cards, Banker draws on 0 to 5 and stands on 6 or 7, the same as Player.

You never have to remember this table, because the dealer (or the software) applies it automatically. It matters for one reason: the Banker hand acts second and gets to react to the Player's third card. That small information advantage is why the banker hand wins slightly more often.

Banker, player and tie odds compared

We worked out every possible deal from a full eight-deck shoe, which is the most common format. Here is how often each outcome happens:

With six decks the figures barely change: banker 1.06%, player 1.24%, tie at 8 to 1 about 14.4%. House edge here is measured per hand dealt, counting ties as pushes.

Why the banker bet pays a commission

If ties are left out, the banker hand wins about 50.7% of the time and the player hand about 49.3%. If the banker bet paid even money, players would have an edge over the casino. The 5% commission takes that advantage back and a little more, leaving the banker bet with a slightly lower house edge than the player bet. Paying commission feels like a cost, but the banker bet still comes out ahead.

Why the tie bet is so expensive

A tie comes up about 9.5% of the time, roughly once every 10.5 hands. A fair payout for that chance would be about 9.5 to 1. At 8 to 1, you are paid well short of the true odds, and the gap works out to a house edge of over 14%. That is more than ten times the cost of the banker bet. Even at the less common 9 to 1, the tie is still more than four times as expensive as betting on the banker. No-commission baccarat and side bets

Many tables, especially online and live dealer games, advertise "no commission". The usual rule is that a banker bet pays even money, except when the banker wins with a total of 6, when it pays only half (1 to 2). A banker win on 6 happens about 5.4% of the time in an eight-deck game.

That one rule raises the banker house edge from about 1.06% to about 1.46%. In other words, no-commission baccarat isn't free; the cost is just moved somewhere you are less likely to notice it. On such a table, the banker bet actually costs more than the player bet (1.24%). Always read the payout rules shown on the table before you assume the banker is the better choice.

Side bets work the same way. Take the common "player pair" bet, which wins if the Player's first two cards are the same rank. In an eight-deck game, that happens about 7.47% of the time. At the usual 11 to 1 payout, the house edge is about 10.4%. Side bets come with many different rules and payouts, so check the paytable, but most carry a far higher edge than the main banker and player bets.

Worked example: what each bet costs on average

These are round illustrative numbers, not a prediction. Suppose you bet $10 a hand for 100 hands, a total of $1,000 in bets, all on one type of bet in an eight-deck game.

BetHouse edgeAverage loss on $1,000 of bets
Banker (5% commission)1.06%$10.58
Player1.24%$12.35
Banker, no-commission table1.46%$14.58
Tie at 9 to 14.84%$48.44
Tie at 8 to 114.36%$143.60

The difference between banker and player is under $2 over 100 hands, so it is not worth worrying about much. The difference between either of them and the tie is huge. That is the real lesson of baccarat strategy: avoid the expensive bets, and the rest is about bet size and time.

These are averages. Over 100 hands your actual result could be well above or well below them, because each hand is close to a coin flip. Baccarat can also be dealt quickly, and the more hands you play in an hour, the more you bet in total. Plan your stakes with a bankroll budget rather than with the averages.

Do baccarat systems and scorecards work?

No. No strategy removes the house edge. Many tables show "roads" or scorecards that track past results, and some players look for streaks or chop patterns to decide their next bet. In an online game dealt by a random number generator, each hand is independent of the ones before it. In a real shoe, removing cards does shift the odds very slightly, but the effect is far too small to overcome the house edge on the main bets, and a printed scorecard does not track it anyway.

Betting progressions such as doubling after a loss, or raising your stake after a win, change only how much you bet and when. Every bet still carries the same house edge, so adding them up always gives a total that favours the casino. Progressions can also push your stakes up fast during a losing run, which is when a table limit or your budget stops you. Our roulette odds guide shows the same problem with doubling systems at the wheel.

What you can control is simple: the bet type, the size of each bet, and how long you play.

Common mistakes at the baccarat table

  • Betting the tie because it "is due". Ties don't become more likely after a long run without one. At 8 to 1, it is one of the most expensive bets in the casino.
  • Avoiding the banker bet because of the commission. The commission is already counted in its 1.06% house edge, which is still lower than the player bet.
  • Assuming no-commission means cheaper. The half-pay rule on a banker 6 makes the banker bet more expensive than at a standard table.
  • Following streaks on the scorecard. Past results don't predict the next hand in any useful way.
  • Adding side bets out of habit. A small side bet on every hand can cost more over a session than your main bet.
  • Chasing losses with bigger bets. Raising your stake doesn't change the odds; it only raises how much you can lose.

When to walk away

Decide on a loss limit and a time limit before you sit down, and stop as soon as you reach either one. Walk away if you find yourself raising bets to win back losses, playing with money meant for rent or bills, or staying longer than you planned. A winning streak is also a fine time to stop, since the house edge works against you on every hand you keep playing. Treat baccarat as paid entertainment, not a way to earn.
