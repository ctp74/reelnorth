No-Deposit Bonuses Explained: What the Terms Really Mean

A no-deposit bonus is a small amount of bonus money, free spins or free play that a casino gives you without asking you to deposit first. You can sometimes turn it into withdrawable cash, but only after meeting its terms, and those terms usually cap what you can take out and make the average value much smaller than the headline suggests.

No-deposit offers are popular because they let you try a casino without spending your own money. That part is true. What the advert leaves out is everything between "claim" and "withdraw": wagering, game rules, bet limits, a cap on cashing out, and often a deposit or identity check before any payout. This guide walks through each of those terms, shows how to work out what an offer is worth on average, and explains when it makes sense to skip one. All numbers in the examples are round illustrative figures, not any real offer.

What a no-deposit bonus is (and the three common types)

A no-deposit bonus is a promotion credited when you sign up, verify an account, or enter a code, with no payment needed. It usually comes in one of three forms:

  • Bonus cash. A small balance of bonus money you can use on eligible games. It is not cash: it has to be wagered before it converts.
  • Free spins. A set number of spins on one slot at a fixed bet. Winnings are normally credited as bonus money with wagering. Our free spins guide covers how to value spins in detail.
  • Free play. A larger balance that must be used within a short time window. When the time is up, anything above a set amount is removed and the rest becomes bonus money with wagering.

Because the casino is giving value to people who may never deposit, no-deposit offers tend to have stricter terms than deposit bonuses. Expect higher wagering, a tight maximum cashout, and more conditions attached to withdrawing.

How no-deposit bonus terms work, step by step

Most no-deposit bonuses follow the same path from claim to cash:

  1. Claim. You register and the bonus is credited, sometimes only after you enter a code or confirm your email or phone number.
  2. Play eligible games. The terms list which games you can use. Some games are excluded entirely; others count for only part of each bet.
  3. Meet the wagering. You place bets totalling a set multiple of the bonus (or of the winnings, for spins) before the balance can convert.
  4. Stay within the rules. A maximum bet per spin or hand applies while the bonus is active, and the whole thing has a deadline.
  5. Convert and withdraw. If you finish the wagering with money left, the balance converts to cash, up to the maximum cashout. Many casinos then ask for identity documents, and some ask for a first deposit, before paying out.

Most of the value is decided in steps 3 and 5. Two offers with the same headline amount can be worth very different sums once you read those terms.

Wagering on a no-deposit bonus: what it costs on average

Wagering is the total you must bet before bonus money becomes cash. If you get a $10 bonus with 40x wagering, you must place 40 × $10 = $400 in bets. Every one of those bets carries the house edge. On a slot with a 96% return to player (RTP), the average cost is 4 cents per dollar bet, so $400 of bets costs $16 on average, which is more than the $10 you were given.

The RTP of the game matters too. At 40x on $10, the same $400 of wagering costs $24 on average on a 94% RTP slot and $12 on a 97% one. The average also hides wide swings: many balances run out before the wagering is done, while a few hit a win large enough to finish it. Wagering also takes time. $400 of wagering is 800 spins at 50 cents, or 2,000 spins at 20 cents. That is a long session for a small balance, and it is worth knowing before you start.

Game weighting and excluded games

Casinos count games differently towards wagering. Slots usually count in full. Table games such as blackjack or roulette often count 10% or nothing at all, because their lower house edge would make bonuses too easy to clear.

Worked example (illustrative numbers). Say a table game has a 1% house edge but counts only 10% towards wagering. To clear $400 of wagering you would need to bet $4,000, because each $10 bet counts as $1. At a 1% edge, $4,000 of bets costs $40 on average. The same $400 of wagering on a 96% RTP slot costs $16. A low house edge doesn't help if the game barely counts.

Terms also often list excluded slots, often including some of the highest-RTP slots. Bets on excluded games may not count at all, and in some terms playing them can void the bonus. Check the list before you play.

Max cashout: the cap on what you can withdraw

Most no-deposit bonuses have a maximum cashout. This is the most you can convert from the bonus into withdrawable cash, however much you win. Anything above it is removed when the bonus converts.

Worked example (illustrative numbers). You claim a $10 bonus with a $100 max cashout, get lucky, complete the wagering and finish with $180. You can withdraw $100. The other $80 is removed.

A cap cuts more value than it seems to. A slot's average return includes the rare big wins. When everything above a set amount is removed, those big wins can't count in full, so the real average value of the bonus falls below the simple figures in the table above. The lower the cap, the more it takes away.

The fine print that trips people up

  • Maximum bet. While the bonus is active there is a limit per spin or hand. A single bet above it can cancel the bonus and any winnings.
  • Expiry. There is usually a deadline to use the bonus and another to finish the wagering. Unfinished wagering is lost when time runs out.
  • Deposit before withdrawal. Some offers require a first deposit before any no-deposit winnings can be paid out, even though the bonus itself needed none. If that applies, the offer isn't fully "no deposit" in practice.
  • Identity checks. Casinos generally verify your age, name and address before the first withdrawal. If the details on your account don't match your documents, payouts can be delayed or refused, so sign up with your real details.
  • One bonus per person. Terms usually allow one no-deposit bonus per person, household, device or payment method. Opening extra accounts to claim it again breaks the terms and can lead to winnings being withheld.
  • Bonus codes and opt-ins. If an offer needs a code or an opt-in, missing that step at sign-up usually means no bonus, and it often can't be added later.

The general rules on playthrough apply here just as they do to deposit bonuses; our wagering requirements guide covers them in more depth.

A quick checklist before you claim

  • What form is the bonus: bonus cash, free spins or timed free play?
  • How many times must it be wagered, and on what amount?
  • Which games count, how much do they count, and which are excluded?
  • What is the maximum bet while the bonus is active?
  • What is the maximum cashout?
  • Is a deposit or identity verification needed before you can withdraw?
  • How long do you have to use the bonus and finish the wagering?

A no-deposit bonus doesn't change the maths of the games. No strategy removes the house edge, with bonus money or your own. The healthy way to see one of these offers is as a free look at a casino and its games, with any cash at the end as a pleasant surprise rather than an expectation. Common mistakes with no-deposit bonuses

  • Treating the headline amount as cash. Bonus money is worth far less than its face value once wagering and the cap are counted.
  • Skipping the terms. Max bet, excluded games and deadlines are only in the small print, and breaking them can cost you the whole balance.
  • Playing low-weighted games to "save" the balance. If a game counts 10%, you need ten times the bets to clear the same wagering.
  • Depositing to unlock a capped balance. If the most you can ever withdraw is small, adding your own money to keep going rarely makes sense.
  • Chasing the next offer. Moving from one no-deposit bonus to another can quietly turn into regular gambling you didn't plan.

When to walk away

Decide your budget before you claim anything, and don't let a bonus change it. Walk away if the terms push you to deposit to withdraw a small capped amount, if a deadline has you betting faster than you normally would, or if you keep playing past the time you set yourself.
