Cashback and Reload Bonuses: How to Work Out Their Value

A cashback bonus gives you back a percentage of your net losses over a set period, while a reload bonus adds a percentage match to a deposit you make after your first one. To work out what either is worth, take the headline amount and subtract the average cost of any wagering attached to it: cashback with no wagering is close to its face value, while a reload bonus with high wagering is often worth little or nothing on average.

Both offers are aimed at players who already have an account, so they get less attention than welcome bonuses. They are also easier to misread. "10% cashback" sounds like it cuts your losses by a tenth on everything you bet, and "50% reload" sounds like half your deposit for nothing. Neither is quite right. This guide explains how each one is calculated, shows the maths for finding the average value, and lists the terms that change the answer. All amounts in the examples are round illustrative figures, not any real offer.

What a cashback bonus is

A cashback bonus returns part of what you lost over a period, often a day, a week or a month. The terms set four things:

  • The percentage. The share of your net losses that comes back to you.
  • The period. The window over which losses are added up.
  • The cap and minimum. Most offers pay no more than a set amount per period, and some pay nothing below a minimum loss.
  • How it is paid. Either as cash you can withdraw straight away, or as bonus money that has to be wagered first. This makes the biggest difference to its value.

Some casinos call it "insurance" or "loss rebate". The idea is the same: you only get something back if you finish the period behind.

How cashback is calculated

Most terms define net losses as the money you lost on eligible games during the period, minus what you won. A common way to express it is deposits, minus withdrawals, minus the balance left at the end of the period. Some terms leave out bets placed with bonus money, and some leave out certain games, so the definition matters.

Worked example (illustrative numbers). You deposit $200 during the week, withdraw nothing, and finish with $50 in your account. Your net loss is $200 − $50 = $150. At 10% cashback you get $15 back. If the offer had a $10 cap, you would get $10. If you had finished the week with $230, you would be ahead, so there would be no cashback at all.

Notice what the percentage applies to. Ten percent cashback returns ten percent of your net losses, not ten percent of everything you bet. Over a week of play you might bet many times your deposit, but only the amount you end up down counts.

What cashback is really worth on average

Because cashback only pays when you lose, its average value depends on how likely you are to finish behind and by how much. Here is a worked example with a game whose maths is fully known.

Worked example: European roulette. Say you make 200 even-money bets of $1 each on European roulette, which has 18 winning pockets out of 37 for a bet on red. The house edge is 1/37, or about 2.70%, so the average loss on $200 of bets is about $5.41. Working through every possible result:

So in this example, 10% cashback paid as cash lowers the average cost of play by about a sixth. That is a real saving, but it is nowhere near cutting your losses by 10% of everything you bet. The house edge is still there; cashback only shaves a little off it. And the saving only arrives in the sessions where you lose.

Cashback with wagering: the same maths as any bonus

If cashback is paid as bonus money with a wagering requirement, it is a bonus like any other, and the wagering has an average cost. The method is the one in our wagering requirements guide: multiply the amount you must bet by the house edge of the game you play.

Wager-free or low-wagering cashback keeps most of its value. Cashback with 25x or more on a typical slot is worth very little on average, however generous the percentage looks.

What a reload bonus is

A reload bonus is a deposit match for existing players. It works like a welcome bonus, usually with a smaller percentage: for example, a 50% match up to a set amount on your next deposit. The bonus money nearly always has wagering, and often a maximum bet, excluded games and a deadline.

The value of a reload bonus is the bonus amount minus the average cost of the wagering. The key detail is what the wagering multiplies: the bonus alone, or your deposit plus the bonus.

Worked example (illustrative numbers). You deposit $100 and get a 50% reload, so $50 of bonus money. With 30x wagering on the bonus, you must bet 30 × $50 = $1,500. On a 96% RTP slot, that costs $60 on average, which is more than the $50 bonus. If the 30x applied to deposit plus bonus instead, you would need to bet 30 × $150 = $4,500, with an average cost of $180.

One honest caveat: if you were going to bet that much anyway, some of the wagering cost would have happened with or without the bonus, so the offer is better for you than the table suggests. The catch is that wagering targets push many people to bet more, and for longer, than they planned. Judge a reload by the play you would actually do, not by the play the terms require.

Cashback vs reload: which is worth more?

They suit different situations. A reload bonus pays up front whatever happens, but the wagering is the price. Cashback pays nothing if you win and only softens a loss, but when it is paid as cash it has no further cost.

  • Wager-free cashback is simple and keeps its value. It is worth roughly its percentage of whatever you end up losing, up to the cap.
  • A reload with low wagering (well under 25x on the bonus at 96% RTP) can be worth more on average than cashback, at the cost of being tied to bonus rules until you finish.
  • A reload with high wagering, or wagering on deposit plus bonus, is usually worth less than nothing on average. Playing with your own money and no bonus keeps withdrawals simple.

Terms to check before you opt in

  • Net loss definition. Are bets made with bonus money included? Are withdrawals subtracted? Which games count?
  • Period and timing. When does the period start and end, and when is the cashback credited?
  • Cap and minimum. The most you can get back, and the smallest loss that qualifies.
  • Cash or bonus money. If it is bonus money, what is the wagering, the deadline and the maximum bet?
  • Game weighting. Table games often count only partly, or not at all, towards wagering on a reload.
  • Opt-in. Many reloads and cashback offers need a code or an opt-in before you deposit or play, and can't be added afterwards.
  • Bonus-funded play. Some terms exclude cashback on any period where you had an active bonus.

Common mistakes

  • Playing on to "qualify". Cashback is a percentage of a loss. Losing more to get more back always leaves you worse off: at 10%, every extra $10 lost returns $1.
  • Treating cashback as a way to win. It lowers the cost of play a little. No bonus and no strategy removes the house edge, and over time the casino still keeps its share.
  • Ignoring the wagering on a reload. The match percentage is the least important number. The wagering multiple and what it applies to decide the value.
  • Depositing more than planned to get a bigger reload. A larger match brings larger wagering. Set the deposit from your budget first, then see if a bonus fits it. Our bankroll management guide shows how to set a session budget.
  • Forgetting the deadline. Unfinished wagering is lost when the time runs out.

If you notice you are depositing again to claim a reload, or playing longer because cashback makes losses feel smaller, that is the time to stop.
